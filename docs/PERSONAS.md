# NeuroStrip buyer playbook

For the Control Bionics sales and marketing team. Prepared by Kellen Levy, October 2026, as part of the [90-day plan](STRATEGY.md). This is an independent proposal. Control Bionics did not publish it.

The plan targets physical therapy (PT) groups with 5 to 25 clinics. Inside each group, four kinds of yes are needed before a pilot turns into a rollout. This playbook says who gives each one, what they care about, what we say to them, and when we stop.

## Words used in this playbook

| Word | What it means here |
|---|---|
| Buying group | Everyone at a practice with a voice in the purchase: who signs, who gives clinical approval, who runs the pilot and who can slow the decision down. |
| Buyer cluster | Roles grouped for testing so each test reaches a large enough audience: executive, operations and clinical. |
| A/B test | Two versions of a message sent to comparable audiences at the same time, to see which one earns more replies. |
| Pilot | A 30-day trial of NeuroStrip at one clinic, with success measures the clinic defines before it begins. |
| Qualified call | A first sales call that actually takes place, with a contact who passes the five-point checklist in the sales section. |
| Sales-ready lead | A contact who passes the first three checks in the sales section (target group, buying role, a specific request) and is ready for a sales call. |
| Intent data | A signal that people at a practice are reading more than usual about a topic, such as billing software. It flags which practices are actively researching but does not identify individual readers. |
| Researching now | A practice whose staff have read more than usual about topics close to NeuroStrip in recent weeks, according to Bombora intent data. |
| Account-based ads | Advertising served only to the people at a named list of target groups. |
| Holdout half | The half of the target list that receives email but no ads. Comparing it with the test half, which also sees ads, isolates the incremental effect of the advertising. |
| National Provider Identifier (NPI) | The 10-digit identifier every American healthcare provider and practice uses for billing. It lets us locate physical therapy practices in a provider database. |
| Surface electromyography (sEMG) | Measurement of the electrical activity of muscles through sensors placed on the skin. NeuroStrip is a wireless sEMG sensor. |
| Electronic medical record (EMR) | The software a clinic uses for patient notes, scheduling and billing. |
| Salesforce | The system the sales team uses to track every contact, call and deal. It records the source of every lead. |

Job titles are spelled out once and then shortened: chief executive officer (CEO) and chief operating officer (COO). Bombora is the company that sells the "researching now" data, and Propensity is the account-based ad platform in the plan.

## Rules for every conversation

- NeuroStrip is registered as a measurement and biofeedback tool that does not diagnose. Every claim stays inside that. Clinicians make the clinical calls.
- We make no billing or coding promises. The clinic's billing team decides, and Control Bionics will confirm what current customers do.
- Anything about electronic medical record (EMR) integration, data security, support or pilot terms is marked "confirm in week one" until Control Bionics confirms it in writing. The list is at the end of this playbook.
- The hamstring case (force plates showed a 7% gap between legs, NeuroStrip showed a 37% gap in muscle activation) is one athlete's example and is always presented that way.
- Customer names, logos, stories and results are used only after Control Bionics has cleared them. No quotes are written for anyone.
- Size patterns and role descriptions below are planning assumptions. Sales corrects them from real calls, and the playbook is updated monthly.

## The buying group

| Part of the decision | Who usually holds it | What they need before saying yes |
|---|---|---|
| Signs | Clinic owner, or the CEO in larger and investor-backed groups | A clear business case, a pilot with measures they agreed, and a yes from the clinical and operations leads |
| Approves clinically | Chief clinical officer, or the clinical director where there is no chief clinical officer | Confidence the readings are sound, the evidence summary, and a protocol their clinicians accept |
| Runs the pilot | A clinic director | A tool that fits into a normal visit, training, and a simple way to report results |
| Decides rollout and payment, and can block it | COO and practice administrator | Annual price, what it includes, how it fits workflow and records, and written answers on patient data |

Every group hears from at least one contact in the executive or operations cluster (owner, CEO, COO or practice administrator) and one in the clinical cluster (chief clinical officer, clinical director or clinic director).

## Who is there at each size, and who to contact first

### 5 to 10 locations

**Who is usually there.** An owner who is often also the chief executive officer and still treats patients, a clinic director at each clinic, a practice administrator or office manager, and often a clinical director who is also a senior clinician. A separate chief operating officer or chief clinical officer is unusual at this size. Planning assumption, checked against the list in week one.

**Contact first.** The owner as the executive and one clinic director as the clinical contact. In weeks 5 to 8 of the plan these groups are split at random, so half start with the owner and half start with the clinic director, and the second contact hears from us a week later. The practice administrator comes in once either one engages.

**Why.** The owner signs and often treats patients, so a clinical message can land with the person who pays. The clinic director runs the pilot and can vouch for it from the clinic floor, and every group still hears from one executive and one clinical contact.

### 11 to 14 locations

**Who is usually there.** An owner or chief executive officer, a clinical director who leads clinical work, a clinic director at each clinic, and a practice administrator or director of operations. A separate chief operating officer sometimes appears at this size. Planning assumption, checked against the list in week one.

**Contact first.** The owner or chief executive officer as the executive and the clinical director as the clinical contact, with the starting order split at random by group in weeks 5 to 8 of the plan to compare starting with the clinical contact against starting with the executive. The practice administrator or director of operations hears from us before the pilot agreement goes out.

**Why.** At this size the clinical director usually controls equipment spend and whether therapists take up new tools, while the owner or chief executive officer still signs. Operations can slow the paperwork or the rollout, so they get the rollout plan early, and every group still hears from one executive and one clinical contact.

### 15 to 25 locations

**Who is usually there.** A separate chief executive officer, chief operating officer and chief clinical officer become likely, often with an owner or investor group above them, plus clinical directors, practice administrators and a clinic director at each clinic. Planning assumption, checked against the list in week one.

**Contact first.** The chief clinical officer as the clinical contact and the chief executive officer as the executive, with the starting order split at random by group in weeks 5 to 8 of the plan to compare starting with the clinical contact against starting with the executive. The chief operating officer gets the rollout plan as soon as either one engages, and a clinic director is named as pilot lead.

**Why.** Clinical approval, rollout and the signature sit with three different people at this size. The chief clinical officer sets protocol, the chief operating officer can stop a rollout and the chief executive officer signs, so each one needs their own message before the pilot ends.

### The contact-order test

From week 5, the demand generation lead splits groups in each size band at random. Half start with a clinical contact and half start with an executive. The second contact hears from us a week later. At Day 90 we compare qualified calls per 100 groups in each half. If one order is at least 30% ahead, it becomes the default for next quarter's new groups. If not, both orders stay, and we read pilots started per 100 groups at Day 180.

## How we test messages

The seven roles fall into three clusters, so each test reaches enough people. Each cluster tests message A against message B from January 4, read in week 8 and closed by February 15, through the Propensity ad audiences and the outbound email sequences.

| Cluster | Roles | Message A | Message B |
|---|---|---|---|
| Executive | Owner, Chief Executive Officer (CEO) | Clinic owner: Patients who can see their progress at every visit have a reason to keep coming back.<br>CEO: Track plan-of-care completion the same way across every clinic. | Clinic owner: A return-to-sport service you can launch at one clinic, measure, then extend to the rest.<br>CEO: One program you can roll out to every clinic and measure. |
| Operations | Chief Operating Officer (COO), Practice Administrator | COO: Each sensor goes on like a bandage, and the pilot times setup at one clinic.<br>Practice administrator: Setup and administrative time, both measured in the pilot. | COO: One page with the annual price and a rollout plan for each new clinic.<br>Practice administrator: One page that lists everything the annual price includes. |
| Clinical | Chief Clinical Officer, Clinical Director, Clinic Director | Chief clinical officer: Strength tests can miss compensation, when other muscles cover for an injured one.<br>Clinical director: See which muscles are doing the work, live, and coach the next rep.<br>Clinic director: Show patients which muscles are doing the work during the test. | Chief clinical officer: A documented return-to-sport protocol for the whole group.<br>Clinical director: The same return-to-sport steps for every therapist in the group.<br>Clinic director: Run the group's first documented return-to-sport protocol at your clinic. |

Each role hears its own wording of the cluster's message A or B, so the test compares the two ideas while the copy still fits the reader. The full copy for each role is in its section below.

**Rules**

- Contacts inside each cluster are split at random between A and B.
- Each test is read in week 8, once about 150 contacts a side have had each message. If neither message is 30% ahead, it runs to 6 weeks, about 300 a side, and closes by the end of week 10.
- We read two numbers: positive reply rate (people who reply with interest) and meetings booked.
- A message wins only if its positive reply rate is 30% or more above the other and meetings booked do not point the other way. Below that it is a tie: we keep the simpler message and test a new one against it.
- Volumes are small. Outbound reaches 300 contacts a week, which is 50 a week in each of the six test cells. At the planned 1.5% positive reply rate, 150 contacts give about 2 replies a side and six weeks give about 4 or 5. A firm win needs about 10 interested replies a side, about 670 people a message, which this quarter cannot reach. So every win is used for now and tested again in month four with fresh contacts, and a gap of one reply counts as a tie.
- Owner: the demand generation lead runs the tests. Week 8 reads them with sales. Remaining tests close by the end of week 10.

### Owner

**Signs.** Executive cluster. The owner signs the contract and wants to know two things: whether patients who see their progress finish more of their visits, and whether a return-to-sport service sets the group apart from nearby clinics.

**Role in the decision.** Signs the pilot and the group contract. In groups of 5 to 10 locations the owner is often also the chief executive officer and still treats patients (a planning assumption, checked against the list in week one), so the owner hears the clinical case and the business case at the same time. Before saying yes, the owner usually asks a trusted clinic director or clinical director to try it and checks the cost with the practice administrator.

**Other titles.** Owner, Co-Owner, Founder, Managing Partner, President, Principal, Owner and Physical Therapist, Managing Member.

**When this is a separate person.** Every group has one. At 5 to 10 locations the owner usually runs the business and still sees patients. At 15 or more locations the owner may be a founding partner or an investor group that has handed day-to-day running to a chief executive officer, who then signs. Planning assumption, checked against the list in week one.

**Measured on**

- Profit
- Growth in new patients and visits
- Referral relationships with local physicians and coaches
- Keeping good therapists on staff

**What worries them**

- Thin margins on each visit
- Patients who stop coming before they finish their plan of care, the set of visits their therapist prescribed
- Equipment that ends up in a drawer after the first month
- Paying for a program the team does not use

**Signs they are ready to talk**

- Watched at least half of the 90-second video, or visited the pilot page twice in one week
- Opened the economics worksheet and entered the group's own visit numbers
- Bombora data shows people at the group reading more than usual about patient retention, practice marketing, biofeedback or return-to-sport testing. Bombora reports by company and does not name people, and the topic names are checked against Bombora's topic list in week one
- Replied to outbound email, even with a question about price
- A clinic director or clinical director at the same group registered for the weekly live demo
- The group posted therapist jobs or announced a new clinic
- Asked for a meeting at the American Physical Therapy Association's Combined Sections Meeting in Philadelphia, February 4 to 6, 2027

**Bombora research topics to check.** Patient retention, Physical therapy practice marketing, Biofeedback, Return-to-sport testing, Sports medicine services. Bombora reports at the company level and does not name people. Topic names are checked against Bombora's list in week one.

**Message A.** Patients who can see their progress at each visit have a reason to keep coming back. NeuroStrip shows each patient's muscle activation live on an iPad, and the pilot tests whether that shows up in plan-of-care completion at one clinic, measured against that clinic's own numbers from before the pilot.

**Message B.** Start a return-to-sport service at one clinic, measure it for 30 days, then add it to your other clinics. NeuroStrip shows activation on up to four muscles at once next to the strength and hop tests your therapists already run, and keeps a record your clinics can show patients. How records can be shared with referring physicians: confirm in week one.

**Offer.** A short economics worksheet that uses the group's own visit numbers to show what each extra completed visit is worth, with no new billing assumed, then a 20-minute pilot scoping call. Owners in the test half whose groups Bombora shows researching now also get one direct mail box, priced in week one.

**Landing page.** The pilot page. One call to action: book a 20-minute pilot scoping call. The economics worksheet sits on the page with no form in front of it.
